The Psychology of Money

No one’s crazy: Everyone’s money decisions are shaped by their own experiences, such as when they were born, their family and the economy they grew up in. So choices that look irrational to you make sense to them. Luck and risk: Outcomes are never entirely down to skill or to bad decisions. Bill Gates’s school had a computer when almost none did, and his friend Kent Evans, equally talented, died young. Don’t credit success only to effort or failure only to laziness. Never enough: Chasing more makes people take risks that could wipe out what they already have. Rajat Gupta and Bernie Madoff were already rich and lost everything. Know when you have enough. Confounding compounding: Compounding needs time more than high returns. Warren Buffett made over 99% of his wealth after his 50th birthday because he started young and stayed invested for decades. Getting wealthy vs. staying wealthy: Getting wealthy takes risk and optimism, while staying wealthy takes frugality, humility and paranoia. Survival matters most. Tails, you win: A few big wins often drive most of the results. You can be wrong often and still succeed if your few hits are large enough. Freedom: The highest dividend money pays is control over your time, meaning the ability to do what you want, when you want, with whom you want. Man in the Car paradox: People admire the car, not the driver. Buying luxury to impress rarely earns the admiration you expect. Wealth is what you don’t see: Wealth is the money you didn’t spend. Being rich (a high income) is different from being wealthy (assets you keep). Save money: Savings come from a gap between your ego and your income. You can build wealth by spending less, and you don’t need a specific reason to save. Reasonable > rational: Aim to be reasonable, not coldly rational. A strategy you can stick with through bad times beats a perfect one you’ll abandon. Surprise!: History’s biggest events are the ones nobody predicted, so the future will surprise you too. Margin of safety: Leave room for error, and don’t plan on a single forecast coming true. You’ll change: Your goals and tastes at 18 will differ at 30 and 50. Avoid extreme future commitments, and expect to change. Nothing’s free: Market volatility is a fee, not a fine. Paying it is the price of long-term returns. You & me: Players in the market have different goals and time horizons. Don’t copy someone else’s strategy if their game isn’t yours (bubbles form when short-term players set the prices). The seduction of pessimism: Pessimism sounds smarter and gets more attention than optimism, but steady progress adds up over time. When you’ll believe anything: The more we want something to be true, the more we believe stories that make it so. Everyone is vulnerable to appealing narratives. All together now: Be humble about what you know, and have a plan that fits your own situation.
