The Trolley Problem

naveeenaa • CSE-2 • 11/9/2026
3:34
Origin: Philippa Foot introduced it in 1967. Judith Jarvis Thomson later developed it and gave it the name “trolley problem” in the 1970s. Purpose: It sets two ethical frameworks against each other. Utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill): The right action is the one that maximizes overall well-being. Five lives outweigh one, so you should pull the lever. Deontology (Immanuel Kant): Some actions are wrong regardless of their outcome. People must never be used merely as a means to an end, so deliberately killing someone is wrong even if it saves more lives. The lever (switch) version: A runaway trolley will hit five people. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a track where it will hit one person. Most people say they would pull it. The footbridge version: To stop the trolley, you must push a bystander onto the tracks. The numbers are identical (one dies, five live), but most people say no. The surgeon version: A doctor could kill one healthy patient and use their organs to save five dying patients. Almost everyone rejects this. Same maths, different feeling: A pure utilitarian calculation can’t explain why the lever feels acceptable and the push doesn’t. Doing vs. allowing: Actively causing a death feels different from letting one happen. Means vs. side effect: In the lever case, the one person’s death is a side effect of saving the five. In the footbridge case, the person’s body is the tool used to stop the trolley. Doctrine of Double Effect (from Thomas Aquinas): It can be acceptable to cause harm as a foreseen side effect of a good act, but not as the intended means to the good result. Emotion vs. reason: Pushing someone with your own hands triggers a stronger emotional reaction than pulling a lever, which affects our judgment.
